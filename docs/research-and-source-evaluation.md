# Spatial Methods — Research and source evaluation standard

**Version:** 0.1  
**Purpose:** Make research and curation credible, traceable, useful and proportionate to the question.

## 1. Research starts with a question

Before searching, write:
- the curiosity in the language the person used;
- the clearest interpretation of the question;
- alternative meanings or ambiguities;
- what a useful answer or next action would look like;
- the scope and any time sensitivity.

Translate broad curiosities into smaller answerable questions. Keep the original wording so the exploration does not lose the reason it began.

## 2. Search strategy

Use more than one discovery route when the question warrants it:
- primary academic literature and citation trails;
- review papers, textbooks and recognised reference works;
- official standards, specifications and documentation;
- universities, research institutions and public agencies;
- maintained software repositories and technical issue trackers;
- dataset catalogues and data-provider documentation;
- lectures, tutorials and specialist communities;
- expert publications and practitioner case studies.

Search using synonyms, established terminology, alternative spellings, related methods and application-specific terms. Search the references of strong sources as well as search results.

Do not treat search-engine rank, AI summaries, citations count or popularity as proof of quality.

## 3. Source type and appropriate use

| Source type | What it is useful for | Checks and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Original research paper | Methods, data, findings and original claims | Study design, assumptions, sample, limitations, peer-review status and subsequent evidence |
| Review / survey | Field overview, comparison and synthesis | Search coverage, date, selection criteria and possible omissions |
| Textbook / book | Foundations, formal explanations and sustained treatment | Edition, author expertise, date and intended level |
| Official documentation | Product behaviour, APIs, workflows and specifications | Version, scope, vendor perspective and whether claims are independently validated |
| Standard / specification | Agreed definitions, interfaces or procedures | Issuing body, version, status and applicability |
| Dataset | Empirical investigation and reproducibility | Provenance, licence, coverage, resolution, temporal range, quality, bias and access |
| Software / repository | Implementation, tests and reproducible workflows | Maintenance, licence, dependencies, tests, issue history and known limitations |
| Lecture / tutorial | Explanations and guided entry points | Instructor expertise, date, depth, references and whether simplifications are flagged |
| Case study | Contextualised application and operational lessons | Transferability, evidence, selection effects and stakeholder perspective |
| Expert commentary | Interpretation and emerging discussion | Expertise, evidence cited, conflicts and distinction between fact and opinion |

No source type is automatically authoritative for every claim.

## 4. Evaluate sources in context

Assess the following dimensions where relevant:

### Authority
Who created or issued the source? What expertise, institutional responsibility or direct involvement supports its use?

### Evidence
Does it show data, methods, references, tests or a transparent basis for its claims? Can important claims be independently checked?

### Relevance
Does it answer the actual question, or is it merely adjacent? Which part of the exploration does it support?

### Currency
Does the subject change quickly? Is the source still current for the intended use? Preserve original publication dates and note when a check was made.

### Methodological quality
Are assumptions, data, methods, limitations and uncertainty disclosed? Are comparisons fair and reproducible?

### Independence and conflicts
Is the source selling a product, advocating a position or reporting its own work? This does not automatically disqualify it, but readers should know.

### Reuse and access
Can the material be linked, downloaded, adapted or redistributed? Are there paywalls, registration requirements or licence restrictions?

Do not turn these dimensions into a simplistic universal score. Explain the relevant strengths and weaknesses in plain language.

## 5. Resource annotation format

For every resource that materially shapes an exploration, write a concise annotation:

- **What it is:** source type, creator and date.
- **Why it is useful:** specific contribution to the question.
- **Use it for:** the concept, route or task it supports.
- **Assumes:** knowledge, tools or data access required.
- **Limitations:** important scope, currency or evidence caveats.
- **Access and reuse:** paywall, licence or restrictions where known.
- **Checked:** date the link and relevant details were last verified.

A resource list without annotations is not a finished curation deliverable.

## 6. Claims and citation practice

- Cite the source closest to the claim it supports.
- Prefer original sources for technical definitions, empirical findings and specifications.
- Use reviews and textbooks for synthesis and foundations where appropriate.
- Distinguish direct source claims from editorial interpretation.
- Do not cite a source for a claim it does not support.
- Verify quotations against the original and keep them brief.
- Preserve enough bibliographic information for another person to locate the source.
- If a source cannot be checked, state that limitation rather than implying verification.

## 7. Research synthesis

When sources disagree:
1. State the precise point of disagreement.
2. Compare definitions, assumptions, datasets, methods and time periods.
3. Identify whether the disagreement is empirical, methodological, terminological or interpretive.
4. Represent each credible position fairly.
5. State what the available evidence supports and what remains unresolved.

Do not manufacture consensus or false balance. Weight conclusions according to the strength and relevance of evidence.

## 8. Technical verification

Where an exploration includes code, calculations or procedures:
- specify environment, software and version;
- inspect official documentation and source code where useful;
- run examples and tests before claiming they work;
- record input assumptions, units, coordinate systems and data requirements;
- compare outputs with known results or an independent method when possible;
- include edge cases and failure modes;
- distinguish teaching code from production-ready software;
- record what was not tested.

For datasets, check provenance, licence, spatial and temporal coverage, resolution, coordinate reference system, quality flags and known limitations.

## 9. Licensing, attribution and ethical use

Before publishing or redistributing an asset, check:
- copyright owner and licence;
- permitted commercial use, modification and redistribution;
- attribution wording and required notices;
- dataset terms, privacy or sensitive-location concerns;
- software dependencies and their licences;
- restrictions on figures, maps, screenshots, photographs and copied text.

Linking to a source is not the same as having permission to reproduce it. When rights are unclear, link and describe rather than copy, or seek permission.

## 10. Research notes and provenance

For substantial investigations, preserve:
- question and scope;
- search date and useful search terms;
- key sources reviewed;
- sources selected and rejected with brief reasons where helpful;
- important claims and citations;
- uncertainties and disagreements;
- experiments or verification performed;
- decisions and changes in interpretation;
- next research questions.

This record makes later updates and corrections faster and more reliable.

## 11. Freshness and review

Assign review priority according to how quickly the subject changes:
- **Stable foundations:** review when a correction, new evidence or meaningful revision is identified.
- **Evolving methods and research:** review periodically and when significant work appears.
- **Software, APIs and datasets:** verify against current versions and provider documentation before recommending for active use.
- **News and releases:** date explicitly; treat as time-sensitive rather than timeless reference material.

Do not claim that an entire resource is current merely because its link still works.

## 12. Minimum publication gate

Before a curated exploration or research dispatch is published:
- [ ] The question and scope are explicit.
- [ ] Important terms and ambiguities are resolved or disclosed.
- [ ] Sources are relevant and their selection is explained.
- [ ] Important claims have traceable support.
- [ ] Disagreements and uncertainty are represented fairly.
- [ ] Resource dates and access limitations are visible where relevant.
- [ ] Technical steps are verified to the level claimed.
- [ ] Licences, attribution and data restrictions are checked.
- [ ] The next useful action is clear.
- [ ] The content has a review or update path.

Quality is not measured by the number of links. It is measured by whether the curation helps someone make a better-informed next move.
