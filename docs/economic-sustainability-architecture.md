# Spatial Methods — Economic sustainability architecture

**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Working architecture  
**Purpose:** Design a financially sustainable ecosystem in which research, learning, practical work, products and partnerships help fund continued exploration and development.

## 1. Economic ambition

Spatial Methods should progressively fund its own operations and growth. It should not depend indefinitely on personal subsidy, unpaid overwork, one platform, one customer or a single revenue stream.

Self-funding is a destination to validate, not an assumption that every activity will immediately pay for itself.

The economic system should support the intellectual mission without turning every piece of knowledge into a paywall or allowing short-term monetisation to distort research quality.

**Operating principle:** Create genuine value, preserve reusable assets, find appropriate ways to capture part of that value, and reinvest a deliberate share into the capabilities that make future work possible.

## 2. What the ecosystem can create value from

One investigation may create several useful assets, but each requires its own validation and maintenance.

| Asset or capability | Potential economic expression |
|---|---|
| Research and synthesis | Sponsored research, commissioned briefs, memberships |
| Explanations and teaching resources | Guides, courses, workshops, memberships |
| Algorithms and software | Licensing, paid downloads, SaaS, APIs, support |
| Datasets and models | Data products, subscriptions, licensing, commissioned data work |
| Simulations and notebooks | Paid learning resources, software, technical services |
| Hardware designs and prototypes | Kits, plans, components, integration and engineering |
| Practical expertise | Training, implementation, consulting and technical support |
| Community and collaboration | Memberships, events, cohort programmes and partnerships |
| Audience and distribution | Customer acquisition, sponsorship and appropriate referrals |
| Research infrastructure | Shared projects, grants, institutional partnerships and paid access |

These are possible business models, not guaranteed revenue. Validate demand, willingness to pay, delivery cost, legal rights and ongoing maintenance before investing heavily.

## 3. Revenue portfolio

Build a diversified portfolio in stages.

### A. Membership and recurring support
- Patreon or another membership platform.
- Guided learning and research experiences.
- Practical companion material and workshops.
- Community participation and bounded group feedback.

Membership should pay for meaningful additional guidance, convenience, participation and original work—not artificial scarcity or a promise of unlimited personal service.

### B. Digital products and software
- Technical guides, workbooks and teaching packs.
- Algorithms, libraries, tools and applications.
- APIs, hosted services and subscriptions.
- Models, datasets and reproducible workflows.

Prefer products that solve a clearly identified problem and can be maintained at a sustainable cost.

### C. Education and events
- Paid workshops and short courses.
- Practical labs and cohort-based programmes.
- Institutional training and professional development.
- Specialist demonstrations and technical clinics.

Set explicit scope, capacity, prerequisites and outcomes.

### D. Physical tools and practical systems
- Experiment kits and open-hardware adaptations.
- Instrument configuration and integration.
- Prototypes, components and build documentation.
- Equipment access, fieldwork and practical training.

Do not purchase expensive equipment speculatively. Begin with simulations, low-cost experiments, existing equipment, borrowing, hiring and partnerships. Acquire or manufacture equipment when demonstrated demand and the economics justify it.

### E. Services and commissioned work
- Research and technical investigations.
- Data engineering and analysis.
- Software and instrument integration.
- Prototyping, evaluation and implementation.
- Specialist consulting and training.

Services can finance product development, but must be scoped so bespoke work does not consume all capacity or create unmaintainable commitments.

### F. Partnerships and external funding
- University and laboratory collaborations.
- Research grants and fellowships.
- Industry sponsorship and commissioned research.
- Equipment loans, in-kind support and shared infrastructure.
- Manufacturer, institution and community partnerships.

Grants and sponsorships can be valuable but are not interchangeable with earned revenue. Record restrictions, reporting obligations, intellectual property terms and renewal uncertainty.

## 4. The value and reinvestment loop

The economic cycle is:

1. Explore a question or problem.
2. Produce knowledge, methods, experiments or tools.
3. Preserve reusable assets and evidence.
4. Identify who benefits and what they might pay for.
5. Select a suitable revenue model and test demand.
6. Deliver and support the offer.
7. Measure revenue, direct cost, maintenance burden and outcomes.
8. Reinvest a deliberate share in operations, research, equipment, products and contributors.
9. Use the improved capability to undertake more valuable work.

Not every exploration needs to be commercialised. The portfolio as a whole must support the mission, while some foundational work may be funded by products, memberships, services or partnerships elsewhere in the system.

## 5. Open knowledge and paid value

Keep the public technical archive independently useful. Do not deliberately degrade free material to force conversion.

Potentially open:
- core explanations and foundational references;
- public code and reproducible examples where licensing permits;
- research notes and corrections;
- selected datasets and experiment records;
- discovery content and entry-level orientation.

Potentially paid:
- structured guidance and curated learning experiences;
- original advanced companions and workbooks;
- workshops, clinics and bounded feedback;
- hosted software, APIs and managed services;
- specialist datasets, premium models or licensed assets;
- equipment access, kits, implementation and support.

The distinction should be based on additional value, cost, rights and service obligations—not an arbitrary division between basic and advanced knowledge.

## 6. Product and activity decision framework

Before investing in an offer, document:

1. **Beneficiary:** Who has the problem or wants the outcome?
2. **Need:** What job, question or capability does it address?
3. **Evidence:** What indicates real demand?
4. **Offer:** What exactly is delivered, and what is excluded?
5. **Alternatives:** What do people use now, including free alternatives?
6. **Pricing:** What will the customer pay, and why?
7. **Acquisition:** How will relevant people discover it?
8. **Delivery:** What time, expertise, infrastructure and support are required?
9. **Economics:** What are the direct costs, contribution and likely maintenance burden?
10. **Rights and risk:** What licences, privacy, safety, legal and intellectual-property issues apply?
11. **Reuse:** What durable asset or capability remains after delivery?
12. **Stop or scale rule:** What evidence would justify continuing, changing or stopping?

Do not confuse audience interest, downloads, likes or expressions of support with demonstrated willingness to pay.

## 7. Unit economics and financial control

Track economics at the offer and activity level.

Core measures:
- gross revenue;
- refunds, taxes and payment/platform fees;
- direct delivery and fulfilment costs;
- contribution after direct costs;
- acquisition cost where measurable;
- delivery hours and effective hourly return;
- recurring hosting, support and maintenance costs;
- retention, repeat purchase and renewal;
- cash required before revenue is received.

Use contribution margin as a practical starting point:

**Contribution = revenue − variable costs directly attributable to delivery.**

Then account for fixed operating costs, owner compensation, taxes, reserves and reinvestment before calling an activity profitable.

Do not treat gross sales, grant awards, pledges or unpaid labour as free cash.

## 8. Reinvestment categories

Maintain transparent internal accounts or budget categories for:

- **Operations:** hosting, software, payment fees, administration and compliance.
- **Research:** data, literature, computing, specialist input and research time.
- **Equipment:** components, instruments, maintenance, calibration and safe storage.
- **Product development:** engineering, testing, design and product maintenance.
- **Contributors:** research, development, teaching, editorial and technical compensation.
- **Distribution:** publishing, marketing, events and customer acquisition.
- **Reserves:** cash buffer for continuity, refunds, renewals and unexpected costs.

Do not prescribe fixed percentages before actual revenue, costs and volatility are known. Review allocations periodically and publish an internal rationale for major spending.

## 9. Asset ownership and stewardship

Treat reusable assets as a growing portfolio:
- original research and documented methods;
- code, software, APIs and product designs;
- datasets, models and experiment protocols;
- teaching material and publications;
- hardware prototypes and build documentation;
- brand, distribution channels and customer relationships;
- partnerships, access arrangements and operational knowledge.

For every asset, record ownership or rights, contributors, licence, provenance, dependencies, maintenance owner and commercial permissions.

Use third-party material only under appropriate licences or permissions. Contributor agreements should make compensation, attribution, ownership, reuse and revenue sharing clear before work begins.

## 10. Cost and capital discipline

- Prefer low fixed costs and reversible commitments in the early stages.
- Use open-source tools and public infrastructure where they meet the need.
- Prototype before purchasing specialist equipment.
- Borrow, rent, partner or use remote facilities when ownership is not justified.
- Avoid building custom platforms before the workflow and demand are validated.
- Do not promise recurring services until delivery capacity and costs are understood.
- Separate personal spending from project spending.
- Maintain a cash forecast and record financial obligations.
- Treat safety, compliance, insurance and maintenance as real costs.

## 11. Portfolio balance

Maintain a deliberate mix of:
- **near-term cash generators:** clear, narrow offers that can be sold and delivered now;
- **compounding products:** reusable software, guides, datasets and tools;
- **capability investments:** research, infrastructure, equipment and specialist skills;
- **long-horizon exploration:** valuable questions whose commercial application is not yet known.

A healthy portfolio does not require every project to generate immediate revenue. It does require visibility into what is funding each activity and why the investment is justified.

## 12. Measures of sustainability

Review monthly or quarterly:
- revenue by stream and offer;
- contribution and operating costs;
- recurring revenue, churn and customer concentration;
- cash runway and committed liabilities;
- sales conversion and repeat purchase;
- delivery effort and support burden;
- proportion of revenue dependent on one platform or customer;
- assets created, reused and maintained;
- experiments and products that reach real users;
- equipment utilisation and total cost of ownership;
- contributor compensation and partnership outcomes.

Measure the quality of the ecosystem, not just audience size or publication volume.

## 13. Implementation sequence

1. Inventory current channels, assets, costs, offers and obligations.
2. Establish a simple financial baseline and monthly reporting sheet.
3. Classify existing and planned activities as revenue, asset creation or strategic capability.
4. Identify a small number of offers with plausible near-term demand.
5. Validate customer problems and willingness to pay before building extensively.
6. Define product delivery, support, licensing and maintenance responsibilities.
7. Test one recurring and one transactional revenue path.
8. Establish reinvestment and reserve rules based on actual cash flow.
9. Add physical equipment and laboratory commitments only when access, demand and cost are understood.
10. Expand partnerships and contributor participation with clear agreements.

## 14. Guardrails

- Never claim that an offer, membership tier, price or partnership is live until verified.
- Never treat a prepared draft or roadmap item as a sale, customer commitment or published benefit.
- Do not promise guaranteed results, mastery, research novelty or production readiness without evidence.
- Do not sacrifice scientific integrity, safety or licensing to increase revenue.
- Do not use customer or member data beyond clear, lawful and disclosed purposes.
- Do not allow sponsorship to silently dictate research conclusions.
- Do not scale costs faster than validated demand and cash capacity.

## 15. Acceptance tests

The economic architecture is usable when:
- each major activity has a clear economic or strategic justification;
- open knowledge and paid value are deliberately distinguished;
- multiple revenue streams can support the mission without depending on one;
- each offer has a beneficiary, scope, price logic, delivery cost and validation plan;
- equipment and infrastructure investments have access and cost alternatives;
- financial performance can be assessed beyond gross revenue;
- reinvestment, reserves, rights and contributor compensation are addressed;
- commercial activity strengthens rather than distorts the knowledge mission.
